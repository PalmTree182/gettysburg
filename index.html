    Saturday, November 14

    The park opens its roads from 30 minutes before sunrise to 30 minutes after sunset. On the 14th the sun rises at about 6:50 AM and sets at about 4:50 PM, so the field is open from roughly 6:20 AM to 5:20 PM. That gives you 10 hours of daylight. It is the real limit on a full tour.

    The campaign in one page

    After Chancellorsville in May 1863, Lee reorganized his army into three corps under Longstreet, Ewell and A. P. Hill, Stonewall Jackson having died of his wounds, and took it north. He meant to draw the war out of Virginia for a season, feed his army on Pennsylvania's harvest, upset Union plans for the summer and, if the chance came, beat the Army of the Potomac on Northern soil.

    Ewell cleared the Shenandoah Valley at Second Winchester in mid-June, and by the end of the month Confederates were on the Susquehanna. Stuart took three cavalry brigades around the Union army on June 25 and was out of touch for a week. Lee did not learn that the Federals had crossed the Potomac until a scout named Harrison reported it on the night of June 28. That same day Lincoln replaced Joseph Hooker with George G. Meade.

    Lee ordered his scattered corps to gather east of South Mountain, near Cashtown and Gettysburg. Meade moved north on a broad front, ready to fight behind Pipe Creek in Maryland if attacked. Ten roads met at Gettysburg. On June 30 Buford's cavalry rode in from the south and found Confederates to the west.

    July 1

    A meeting engagement that neither commander planned. Heth and Pender from the west and Rodes and Early from the north drive the I and XI Corps through the town. Reynolds is killed. The Union rallies on Cemetery Hill and Ewell does not attack it. About 9,000 Union and 6,000 Confederate casualties.

    July 2

    Meade holds a three-mile fishhook: Culp's Hill, Cemetery Hill, Cemetery Ridge, the Round Tops. His reserves can cross from one end to the other in minutes. Lee attacks both ends. Longstreet's two divisions strike the Union left at 4 pm, wreck Sickles's advanced III Corps at the Peach Orchard, Wheatfield and Devil's Den, and are stopped at Little Round Top and on Cemetery Ridge. At dusk Ewell attacks Culp's Hill and East Cemetery Hill and is held. It is the costliest day, with about 20,000 casualties.

    July 3

    The XII Corps retakes its works on Culp's Hill by 11 am. At 1 pm the Confederate cannonade begins. At 3 pm about 12,500 men under Longstreet's direction attack the Union centre and are thrown back with the loss of more than half. Stuart is checked three miles to the east.

    Afterward

    Lee holds Seminary Ridge through July 4, then retreats in the rain with a train of wounded 17 miles long. Vicksburg surrenders to Grant the same day. Lee crosses the Potomac on July 13 and 14. Lincoln believes Meade has let him escape and drafts a letter saying so, which he never sends.

    The numbers. Union: about 93,900 engaged and 23,049 casualties (3,155 killed, 14,529 wounded, 5,365 missing or captured). Confederate: about 71,700 engaged. Lee's army reported some 23,000 losses; modern estimates run to 28,000. Every figure in this guide is an estimate from the sources listed, and historians differ on most of them.

    Stories to hold loosely

    • The battle began over shoes. Heth said so after the war. There was no shoe factory, and Lee was already concentrating here.
    • Buford's men had repeating rifles. They had single-shot breech-loading carbines.
    • Jackson would have taken Cemetery Hill. A postwar verdict. Gallagher shows how reasonable Ewell's decision looks from where he stood.
    • Longstreet lost the battle by sulking. Built by Jubal Early and others after Longstreet criticized Lee and turned Republican.
    • Chamberlain alone saved the Union left. Warren, Vincent, O'Rorke and Hazlett did as much. Chamberlain wrote more, and a novel did the rest.
    • It was Pickett's charge. Longstreet commanded it, and Pickett led fewer than half the men.
    • The copse of trees was the High Water Mark of the Confederacy. The phrase and the monument are John Bachelder's, from the 1880s and 1890s. Few Confederates in 1863 thought the war had turned here.
    • Stuart was to hit the Union rear as Pickett hit the front. No order or report says so.
    • The dead sharpshooter in Devil's Den. The photographers moved and posed the body.
    • Enemies shared Spangler's Spring. No account from 1863 mentions it.

    Read before Saturday

    If you have one evening

    1. The Park Service's virtual tour, built on the same 16 stops you will drive.
    2. The American Battlefield Trust's animated map of the three days.
    3. Frank Haskell's letter (below). It is the best eyewitness account of July 3 from the Union wall and it reads in an hour.

    Gary Gallagher, in this order

    Gallagher edited essay volumes on the first and second days, later combined with new third-day essays as Three Days at Gettysburg (Kent State, 1999), and a separate volume, The Third Day at Gettysburg and Beyond (UNC, 1994). Essays 1 to 4 are in the first book and 5 and 6 in the second. Read them in this order:

    1. Gallagher, "Confederate Corps Leadership on the First Day at Gettysburg: A. P. Hill and Richard S. Ewell in a Difficult Debut." For stops 1 to 6.
    2. A. Wilson Greene, "From Chancellorsville to Cemetery Hill: O. O. Howard and Eleventh Corps Leadership." For Barlow Knoll and Cemetery Hill.
    3. Gallagher, "'If the Enemy Is There, We Must Attack Him': R. E. Lee and the Second Day at Gettysburg." For Warfield Ridge.
    4. Robert K. Krick on Longstreet and William Glenn Robertson on Sickles, both in the second-day essays. Two opposed readings of the same afternoon.
    5. Gallagher, "Lee's Army Has Not Lost Any of Its Prestige: The Impact of Gettysburg on the Army of Northern Virginia and the Confederate Home Front." Read it before you stand at the Angle.
    6. Carol Reardon and William Garrett Piston, in The Third Day at Gettysburg and Beyond, on how Pickett's Charge was planned and how it was remembered.

    Gallagher also edited the best Confederate memoir of the battle, E. P. Alexander's Fighting for the Confederacy. Alexander ran Longstreet's guns on July 3 and wrote with unusual candour.

    One full narrative

    • Stephen W. Sears, Gettysburg (2003). The most readable single volume.
    • Edwin B. Coddington, The Gettysburg Campaign: A Study in Command (1968). Still the standard study.
    • Harry W. Pfanz's three books, one per sector, when you want the regiments.

    Voices from 1863

    • Lt. Frank A. Haskell, The Battle of Gettysburg. A staff officer at the Angle, writing to his brother two weeks later.
    • Lt. Col. Arthur Fremantle, Three Months in the Southern States, entries for July 1 to 4. A British observer beside Longstreet and Lee.
    • The reports of Meade, Lee, Longstreet, Hancock, Hunt and Chamberlain in the Official Records, series I, volume 27. The Park Service posts many of them.
    • Henry J. Hunt's three articles in Battles and Leaders of the Civil War, volume 3.
    • Tillie Pierce, At Gettysburg, or What a Girl Saw and Heard of the Battle. The town, from a 15-year-old.
    • Lincoln's unsent letter to Meade of July 14, 1863, and the Address.

    Read knowing what it is

    Michael Shaara's novel The Killer Angels (1974) and the film made from it are fine storytelling built largely on Longstreet's and Chamberlain's own memoirs. They are the source of much of what visitors expect to see. Enjoy them, and check them against the reports.

    Limits of this guide

    • Stop positions are the monument or landmark locations and are accurate to a few yards. The spot where Reynolds fell and the Bliss farm site are placed by description and marked approximate.
    • Troop lines and arrows on the map show the general position of each army in that phase. They are not a survey.
    • The relief, the 3D model and the drawn ground in Look come from NASA's SRTM elevation survey, about 30 metres between points. They show the shape of the land, not trees, fences or buildings. A label marked "behind high ground" is hidden according to that data.
    • In Look, the labels are placed by compass bearing from where you stand. Trees and ridges will hide some of what they point to.
